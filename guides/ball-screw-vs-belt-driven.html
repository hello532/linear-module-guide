Ball Screw vs Belt Driven Linear Module: How to Choose

Both move a load along one axis. They fail in different places, and that is what decides the choice.

Short answer. Choose a ball screw module when you need repeatability tighter than ±0.02 mm, a vertical axis, or a high thrust push. Choose a belt driven module when stroke is over 1.5 m, cycle speed matters more than precision, or the axis is horizontal transfer. At strokes under 300 mm the cost gap is small, so decide on accuracy and orientation instead.

Why ball screws are more accurate

A ball screw converts motor rotation into linear travel through a rigid metal thread. The path from motor to carriage has almost no elastic element, so commanded position and actual position stay close. Lead error is a machining property of the screw and can be graded and compensated.

A toothed belt is elastic by design. Under acceleration or a sudden load change, the belt stretches slightly and the carriage lags, then overshoots. That elasticity is also why belt drives tolerate shock better, but it puts a floor under achievable repeatability.

Why belt drives win on long stroke

A rotating screw has a critical speed. Above it the screw whips and vibrates, and that speed falls as the screw gets longer. A 2 m screw must therefore run slower than a 0.5 m screw, which is the practical ceiling on long ball screw axes.

A belt has no such limit. Lengthening the axis adds belt and profile, not rotating mass, so speed stays roughly constant. Cost scales the same way, which is why gantries and transfer axes are usually belt driven.

The vertical axis question

This one is a safety decision, not a preference. A belt driven carriage back-drives when power is cut: the load falls. A ball screw with a lead of 5 mm or less has enough internal friction to resist back-driving, which is why Z axes on pick and place machines are usually ball screw driven. If a belt drive must be used vertically, a motor brake is not optional.

Three specs to fix before you ask for a quote

Most quoting delays come from missing these:

1. Stroke, and whether it is fixed

Effective stroke, not overall length. A custom stroke is normal on both types; say the number and the tolerance you can live with.

2. Orientation and load

Horizontal, vertical, or wall mounted, plus the moving mass and where its centre of gravity sits relative to the carriage. An offset load changes the bearing selection.

3. Duty cycle

Cycles per hour and hours per day. A module that is fine at 200 cycles per hour may need a larger screw or a wider belt at 2000.

Frequently asked

Which is more accurate?

Ball screw. Typical repeatability is ±0.01 to ±0.02 mm against ±0.05 to ±0.1 mm for belt driven, because the belt stretches under load.

Can a belt driven module be mounted vertically?

Only with a brake or counterweight. It is not self-locking and will back-drive on power loss.

What is the maximum practical stroke?

Belt driven is practical to 6 m and beyond. Ball screw is usually capped near 1.5 to 2 m by critical speed.

Which is cheaper?

Belt driven per unit of stroke, and the gap widens with length. Under 300 mm they are close enough that accuracy should decide.
