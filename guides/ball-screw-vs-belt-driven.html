Ball screw vs belt driven

Both move a load along one axis. They differ in where the accuracy comes from and how the axis behaves as it gets longer.

Short answer. When the axis gets long and speed matters more than micron precision, choose a belt driven module. When precision comes first, choose a ball screw module. A screw whips as it gets longer, which is the practical reason long transfer axes are usually belt driven.

Why precision favors the ball screw

A ball screw converts motor rotation into travel through a rigid metal thread, so the path from motor to carriage has almost no elastic element. KINX publishes repeatability of 0.003–0.01 mm, and KK60 and KK86 are available in C and P accuracy grades. A toothed belt is elastic by design: under acceleration the belt stretches slightly and the carriage lags, which puts a floor under achievable repeatability.

Why long stroke favors the belt

A rotating screw has a critical speed: past it, the screw whips and vibrates. The longer the screw, the lower that critical speed becomes, so a long ball screw axis must run slower. A belt does not have that failure mode — lengthening the axis adds belt and profile, not a long rotating shaft. That is why long transfer axes and gantries are usually belt driven, while short precision axes are ball screw.

Calculating axis speed

For both drive types, linear speed comes from how fast the motor turns and how far one revolution moves the carriage (the lead):

linear speed (mm/s) = motor speed (rpm) ÷ 60 × lead (mm)

Example: a motor at 3000 rpm on a 10 mm lead gives 3000 ÷ 60 × 10 = 500 mm/s. The lead is the number you trade against thrust and resolution — a larger lead moves faster per revolution, a smaller lead gives finer positioning per step.

Accuracy grades, C and P

KK60 and KK86 ball screw modules come in C and P grades. The grade figures (all in mm):

GradeRepeatabilityPositioning accuracy
C grade KK60±0.005 mm0.04–0.07 mm
C grade KK86±0.0055 mm0.04–0.07 mm
P grade KK60±0.003 mm0.02–0.025 mm
P grade KK86±0.003 mm0.025–0.04 mm

Repeatability is how closely the axis returns to the same commanded point; positioning accuracy is how close that point is to the true coordinate. Assembly machines usually care about repeatability first, because a fixed offset can be taught out.

Three things to fix before asking for a quote

1. Stroke, and whether it is fixed

Effective stroke, not overall length. Custom lengths are routine on KK60 and KK86; KK50 takes standard strokes only.

2. Orientation and load

Horizontal or vertical — both mounting orientations are supported. State the moving mass and where its centre of gravity sits relative to the carriage, because an offset load changes the bearing selection.

3. Speed and duty cycle

Give the speed you need at the carriage and the cycles per hour, hours per day. Together with the lead, that fixes the motor side of the selection. Motor adaptation: KK50 servo 50/100 W, stepper 42; KK60 servo 50/100/200/400 W, stepper 42/57/60; KK86 servo 50/100/200/400/750 W, stepper 57/60/86.
