What is a linear module?

The short version: someone already did the alignment work for you.

Definition. A linear module is a pre-assembled single-axis actuator. It packages a linear guide, a drive element (a ball screw or a toothed belt), a carriage and a housing into one unit with a defined stroke and a defined accuracy. You bolt on a motor and a load. The alternative is buying a rail, blocks, a drive, end supports and a coupling, then machining a base and aligning it all yourself.

What is inside one

  • Housing — an aluminium or steel profile that carries the load path and keeps everything parallel.
  • Linear guide — a rail and bearing blocks that constrain the carriage to one axis and absorb side and moment loads.
  • Drive element — a ball screw or a toothed belt, which turns motor output into travel.
  • Carriage — the moving plate with a mounting pattern for your tool.
  • End blocks and support seats — bearing housings that locate the screw and take axial load.
  • Coupling and motor mount — connects your motor, usually through an adapter for common servo or stepper flanges.

Buying it assembled matters because parallelism between the rail and the screw is the hard part. Get it wrong and the nut side-loads, friction rises and life drops. A module ships with that alignment already held to a documented tolerance.

Linear module vs bare linear guide

These get confused constantly, and the distinction is simple.

Bare linear guideLinear module
What it doesConstrains motion to one axisProduces motion along one axis
Has a drive?NoYes — ball screw or belt
Needs external actuation?Yes, something else must push itNo, fit a motor and it moves
LevelComponentSubsystem
You still designBase, drive, alignment, end supportsMotor selection and tool mounting

Put plainly: a bare guide is a rail; a module is a rail that already knows how to move.

The main types

Ball screw driven

A screw drives a nut fixed to the carriage. This is the precision-first choice: KINX publishes repeatability of 0.003–0.01 mm, and KK60 and KK86 come in C and P accuracy grades. The KK series covers KK50, KK60 and KK86.

Belt driven

A toothed belt moves the carriage between two pulleys. Built for long transfer strokes and high speed; accuracy is lower than a ball screw, because the belt is elastic under load.

Embedded modules

The screw runs inside the slide table, keeping the axis compact. KINX builds the GTH5/8 and GTH12 series.

Multi-axis systems

Two or three single-axis modules combine into multi-axis systems — XY, XYZ, or cantilever configurations. See multi-axis systems on the product site.

What the published specs mean

Repeatability is how closely the axis returns to the same commanded point. Positioning accuracy is how close that point is to the true coordinate. KINX publishes positioning accuracy of ±0.01 mm and repeatability of 0.003–0.01 mm. The C and P grade figures for KK60 and KK86 are in the sizing guide.
