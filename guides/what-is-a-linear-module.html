What Is a Linear Module?

The short version: someone already did the alignment work for you.

Definition. A linear module is a pre-assembled single-axis actuator. It packages a linear guide, a drive element (usually a ball screw or a toothed belt), a carriage and a housing into one unit with a defined stroke and a defined accuracy. You bolt on a motor and a load. The alternative is buying rails, blocks, a screw, support seats and a coupling, then machining a base and aligning it all yourself.

What is inside one

  • Housing — an extruded aluminium or steel profile that carries the load path and keeps everything parallel.
  • Linear guide — a rail and bearing blocks that constrain the carriage to one axis and absorb side and moment loads.
  • Drive element — a ball screw, a toothed belt, or a linear motor forcer, which turns motor output into travel.
  • Carriage — the moving plate with a mounting pattern for your tool.
  • End blocks and support seats — bearing housings that locate the screw and take axial load.
  • Coupling and motor mount — connects your motor, usually with an adapter for common NEMA or servo flanges.

Buying it assembled matters because parallelism between the rail and the screw is the hard part. Get it wrong and the nut side-loads, friction rises, and life drops. A module ships with that alignment already held to a documented tolerance.

Linear module vs linear guide

These get confused constantly, and the distinction is simple.

What it doesConstrains motion to one axisProduces motion along one axis

Put plainly: a linear guide is a rail, a linear module is a rail that already knows how to move.

The main types

Ball screw driven

A rotating screw drives a nut fixed to the carriage. Highest accuracy and thrust; repeatability typically ±0.01 to ±0.02 mm, and ±0.005 mm with a precision ground screw. Practical stroke is capped near 1.5 to 2 m because critical speed falls as the screw lengthens. Near self-locking at low lead, which makes it the default for vertical axes.

Belt driven

A toothed belt pulls the carriage between two pulleys. Long stroke (6 m and beyond) and high speed (2 to 5 m/s) with repeatability around ±0.05 to ±0.1 mm, because the belt is elastic. Cost per metre of stroke barely rises with length. Not self-locking, so a vertical axis needs a brake.

Linear motor driven

No mechanical drive at all; a magnet track and a coil forcer produce thrust directly. Highest speed and accuracy, no backlash and nothing to wear in the drive path, at the highest cost. Used in semiconductor handling, inspection and other precision equipment.

Rack and pinion

A pinion on the carriage drives along a fixed rack. Used where travel runs to tens of metres, as on large gantries, at lower precision than the other three.

Where they are used

Assembly machines and pick and place stations, testing and inspection equipment, gantry and Cartesian robots, dispensing and screwdriving stations, laser and plasma cutting tables, 3D printers and additive machines, semiconductor and electronics handling, packaging and labelling lines, and medical laboratory automation.

Two or three modules bolted orthogonally make an XY or XYZ system, which is the usual way a Cartesian robot gets built.

When a module is the wrong answer

It is not always the right buy. If travel is under 50 mm and you only need two positions, a pneumatic cylinder with stops is cheaper and simpler. If the axis carries a huge load at very low precision, a rail and a cylinder may do. And at extremely high volume, an integrated custom design can beat a catalogue module on cost, once the engineering is amortised.

Frequently asked

What is a linear module?

A pre-assembled single-axis actuator combining a guide, a drive element, a carriage and a housing. Fit a motor and a load and it works.

How is it different from a linear guide?

A guide only constrains motion and needs something else to push it. A module contains its own drive.

Which types exist?

Ball screw, belt driven, linear motor, and rack and pinion for very long travel.

Which specs matter most?

Effective stroke and repeatability decide the shortlist; orientation, load offset and duty cycle decide the size.

Where are they used?

Assembly, testing, pick and place, gantries, dispensing, cutting tables, 3D printers, and electronics handling.
