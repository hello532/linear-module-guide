Choosing a Linear Module for Assembly and Testing Machines

Five numbers decide the module. Get them wrong and you either overpay or rebuild the axis six months in.

Short answer. Fix these five before shortlisting: required repeatability (about one third of your placement tolerance), stroke, axis orientation, moving mass with its offset from the carriage, and duty cycle in cycles per hour. For most assembly machines that lands on a ball screw module at ±0.01 to ±0.02 mm; for optical or connector work, a ground screw at ±0.005 mm.

1. Accuracy: work back from your tolerance

Do not start from the module spec sheet. Start from the tolerance the finished product must hold, then give the axis roughly one third of it as a budget. The remainder is consumed by fixturing, tool compliance, thermal drift and the part itself.

Note the distinction the datasheet makes: repeatability is how closely the axis returns to the same commanded point, positioning accuracy is how close that point is to the true coordinate. Assembly machines usually care about the first, because a fixed offset can be taught out. Measurement systems care about both.

2. Orientation drives the drive type

A vertical axis is a safety question. A belt driven carriage back-drives when power is lost and the load falls. A ball screw with a lead of 5 mm or less is close to self-locking, which is why Z axes on pick and place stations are ball screw driven. If the axis is vertical and you still want a belt, budget for a motor brake.

Wall or inverted mounting changes which bearing face carries the load. Say the orientation explicitly rather than sending only a stroke number.

3. Moving mass, and how far off centre it sits

This is the specification most enquiries omit. A 5 kg gripper bolted flat on the carriage is a different load case from the same 5 kg on a 200 mm bracket, because the offset creates a moment the linear guide must absorb. That moment, not the mass, usually decides the guide rail size.

Give three things: moving mass in kg, the offset distance from the carriage face, and whether the load overhangs along the travel axis or across it.

4. Duty cycle sets life, not precision

A module that performs perfectly at 200 cycles per hour can wear out in months at 2000. Duty cycle does not change the accuracy you get on day one; it changes how long you keep it. Above roughly 1500 cycles per hour, expect to size up the screw and nut, add automatic lubrication, or shorten the maintenance interval.

State cycles per hour and hours per day. A machine running 500 cycles per hour across three shifts is a heavier duty case than 1500 cycles per hour for one shift.

5. Stroke: effective, not overall

Effective stroke is the usable travel. Overall length adds the housing, motor mount and end caps, and it is what has to fit your frame. Custom stroke lengths are routine on both ball screw and belt driven modules, so give the travel you need plus the envelope you have.

Worked example: a test station probe axis

A functional tester presses a probe onto a PCB pad at ±0.03 mm, 900 cycles per hour, two shifts. The probe head is 1.2 kg mounted 60 mm forward of the carriage. Travel is 150 mm, vertical.

  1. Tolerance ±0.03 mm → target repeatability about ±0.01 mm → ball screw, not belt.
  2. Vertical → lead 5 mm for near self-locking behaviour.
  3. 1.2 kg at 60 mm offset → small moment, a compact guide size is adequate.
  4. 900 cycles per hour over two shifts → mid duty, specify a lubrication port and a grease interval.
  5. 150 mm effective stroke → short axis, so critical speed is not a constraint.

Result: a compact ball screw module, 5 mm lead, standard accuracy class. No need to pay for a ground screw here, because the tolerance does not demand ±0.005 mm.

Frequently asked

What accuracy do I need?

About one third of your placement tolerance. ±0.1 mm placement → ±0.03 mm repeatability.

How does duty cycle change the choice?

It sets service life. Above roughly 1500 cycles per hour, size up the screw and nut or shorten lubrication intervals.

Ball screw or belt for a probe axis?

Ball screw. On a tester the probe position is part of the measurement, so belt elasticity becomes measurement uncertainty.

Why does the supplier ask about cantilever load?

Because an offset mass creates a moment on the guide. 5 kg at 200 mm loads the rail far harder than 5 kg centred.
