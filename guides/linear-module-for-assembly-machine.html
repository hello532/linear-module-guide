Choosing a linear module for assembly and testing machines

Five questions decide the module. Answer them in order and the shortlist writes itself.

Short answer. Work through: 1. effective stroke, 2. moving load and mounting orientation, 3. speed, 4. required accuracy, 5. environment. Send those five to a supplier and you get a proposal back in 6 hours.

1. Stroke: effective, not overall

Effective stroke is the usable travel; overall length adds the housing, motor mount and end caps, and it is what has to fit your frame. State both: the travel you need and the envelope you have.

So a 500 mm axis is routine: it falls inside both the KK60 and the KK86 range. A 70 mm axis is the smallest KK50 standard stroke, and KK50 takes standard strokes only.

2. Load and orientation

Give the moving mass and where its centre of gravity sits relative to the carriage. A gripper bolted flat on the carriage is a different load case from the same mass on a long bracket, because the offset creates a moment the linear guide must absorb. That moment, not the mass alone, usually decides the guide size.

Mounting orientation matters twice. First, horizontal and vertical mounting are both supported, but they change which bearing face carries the load. Second, a vertical axis is a safety question — state it explicitly rather than sending only a stroke number.

3. Speed

Speed sets the drive and the lead. The relationship between motor and carriage is fixed:

linear speed (mm/s) = motor speed (rpm) ÷ 60 × lead (mm)

A larger lead moves faster per revolution; a smaller lead gives finer positioning per step. Once the speed is known, the motor side of the selection follows from the series. Motor adaptation: KK50 servo 50/100 W, stepper 42; KK60 servo 50/100/200/400 W, stepper 42/57/60; KK86 servo 50/100/200/400/750 W, stepper 57/60/86.

Also state the duty cycle: cycles per hour and hours per day. Duty cycle does not change the accuracy you get on day one; it changes how long you keep it.

4. Accuracy

Do not start from the datasheet; start from the tolerance the finished product must hold, then budget a fraction of it for the axis. The rest is consumed by fixturing, tool compliance, thermal drift and the part itself.

Two different numbers get confused here. Repeatability is how closely the axis returns to the same commanded point. Positioning accuracy is how close that point is to the true coordinate. Assembly machines usually care about repeatability first, because a fixed offset can be taught out. KINX publishes positioning accuracy of ±0.01 mm and repeatability of 0.003–0.01 mm. Where the axis needs a defined grade, KK60 and KK86 ball screw modules are available in C and P grades (mm):

Grade figures and published values are consistent: the standard positioning accuracy of ±0.01 mm sits alongside the grade table, not against it. KINX modules have shipped into vision labeling machines and medical equipment.

5. Environment

The environment decides the enclosure: fully enclosed, semi-enclosed, or bellows-protected. State what the axis will see — dust, chips, glue splash, corrosion risk — and the seal type gets matched to the machine. A clean room and a machining cell take different versions of the same stroke and accuracy.

Worked order: a 500 mm transfer axis

  1. Stroke: 500 mm effective → inside KK60 and KK86 range.
  2. Load and orientation: state the mass and its offset from the carriage; state horizontal or vertical.
  3. Speed: apply the formula and pick the lead; state the duty cycle.
  4. Accuracy: standard positioning accuracy ±0.01 mm and repeatability 0.003–0.01 mm, or a C / P grade if the tolerance demands it.
  5. Environment: dust, chips, glue or corrosion → enclosure type.

Frequently asked

How do I size a linear module?

Five questions in order: stroke, load and orientation, speed, accuracy, environment.

What accuracy can KINX hold?

Positioning accuracy ±0.01 mm, repeatability 0.003–0.01 mm. KK60 and KK86 add C and P grades with tighter repeatability.

How do I calculate speed?

mm/s = rpm ÷ 60 × lead (mm).

Which environment needs which enclosure?

Fully enclosed, semi-enclosed or bellows, decided by dust, chips, glue and corrosion risk.
